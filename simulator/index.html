Dinner Rush Simulator

Move the levers and see what happens to late deliveries and the weekly P&L in a real food-delivery marketplace. An independent case by Jessica Ayivi.

Runs on real DoorDash deliveries from a public Kaggle dataset: 176,448 orders in six anonymised US markets, 21 Jan–17 Feb 2015 (the publisher added noise). It is not Uber data and not Belgian data. The batching delay comes from a second public dataset (Indian cities, 2022). Money is in US dollars.

Levers

1Rebalance shifts

Share of idle dasher time in quiet hours (load under 0.8) moved into stressed hours of the same market, at no cost.

2Batching

Pair orders in stressed hours. A paired order arrives later (measured), and frees courier time.

3Targeted quests

Paid extra hours, only where load after steps 1–2 stays above the trigger. 30% of spend goes to dashers who would have worked anyway.

Blanket weekend bonus

Model. The response curve is the observed share of late deliveries at each load ratio, which bends at : below it each extra 0.1 of load adds under 1 point of lateness, above it nearly 5. Levers act on stressed market-hours (load above ) by adding dashers or stretching them, and the curve gives the lateness avoided. Dashers per market-hour come from Little’s law (orders × hours per delivery ÷ load). The blanket comparison pays an hour to every dasher online on Fri–Sun 17:00–21:00, and 60% of the gap shows up.

How I would use this at Uber Eats Belgium. Rerun the same queries on Belgian city-hours, check whether the knee sits in the same place, and test the levers with a switchback experiment (city × 2-hour blocks).
