Where should couriers wait?

Pick an hour: the map shows which zones are short of couriers and which have spare ones, and the optimiser moves the same couriers to cut late deliveries. By Jessica Ayivi. Evidence behind it: an analysis of 538,689 real delivery orders.

A model, not Uber results. Real public data informs the model: where people live (Statbel), where restaurants are (OpenStreetMap), when it rained (Open-Meteo, Sep–Oct 2026) and how lateness grows when couriers are busy (Meituan's real delivery data). Brussels orders, where couriers wait and how many orders arrive late are modelled, not observed. Uber's data isn't public; nothing here is Uber data.

    How the map works, and where the numbers come from

    The map is built in five steps. Each number is marked measured (taken from real data), calculated (worked out by the map) or guess (my assumption, tested higher and lower).

    1. How many orders, and where.
      • I assume Brussels gets orders a week on one app guess. Uber doesn't publish this number.
      • The orders are spread over the week like a real week of food delivery: few in the morning, many on Friday evening measured. For example, Friday 19:00 gets orders.
      • Each zone's share of those orders depends mostly on how many restaurants it has, and a little on how many people live there: guess.
      • When it rains, there are more orders: 100 orders become guess.
      You see it in "Busiest zones this hour" and when you point at a zone.
    2. How many couriers, and where they wait today.
      • Each order keeps a courier busy for about minutes, from accepting it to delivering it measured.
      • A courier can carry several orders at once. In real delivery data, each courier was carrying about orders at a time at quiet hours, about at 19:00 and up to at the lunch rush measured.
      • So at Friday 19:00: orders × minutes is about hours of work. With each courier carrying about orders at once, that needs about couriers calculated.
      • When it rains, there are more orders but no extra couriers.
      • The assumed baseline ("Assumed today"): couriers wait where their last delivery ended. Most deliveries end at people's homes, so couriers end up spread like the population, with at least 1 courier per zone guess. Uber's actual courier positioning is unknown.
      You see it in the "Assumed today" view and column.
    3. How busy each zone is (the colour).
      • For each zone, the map works out how many orders each courier is handling at the same time calculated.
      • Example: 60 orders × 25 minutes = 25 hours of work. Shared by 10 couriers, each one handles 2.5 orders at once.
      • Grey: under 1.25 orders each (spare couriers). Orange: 1.75 to 2.5 (stretched). Red: over 2.5 (short of couriers).
      You see it as the colour of each zone.
    4. How many orders arrive late.
      • Some orders are late even when couriers have nothing else to do, because of slow kitchens or traffic: about in 100 () measured.
      • The busier couriers are, the more orders are late. In real delivery data, each time couriers became twice as busy, about more orders in every 100 arrived late measured. For example: about 11 late in 100 when each courier carries 1 order, about 15 when they carry 2, about 20 when they carry 4.
      You see it in the "Late orders this hour" box.
    5. Where couriers should go.
      • The map moves the same couriers, one at a time, to the zone where each one prevents the most late orders. A professional maths tool gives exactly the same answer calculated.
      • Couriers choose where they wait, so the platform can only encourage them, for example with a heatmap or a small bonus. I assume in 10 couriers follow that advice guess.
      • A much simpler rule, "put couriers where the restaurants are", does almost as well. At Friday 19:00 it leaves only more late orders than the best placement: about late orders an hour instead of calculated. So the value isn't clever maths: it's moving couriers back towards the restaurants every hour, because they keep drifting away.
      You see it in the "Optimised" view, the "Fewer late orders" and "Couriers to move" boxes, and "Suggested shifts between zones".

    Testing the guesses: I made each guess 30% bigger and 30% smaller. Every time, the map still cut late orders over four weeks by between . So the conclusion doesn't depend on any one guess being exactly right.

    About the number of couriers: the lateness rule and the courier count use the same measure from the real data (couriers who are carrying orders), so they match each other. The real number of couriers online, including those waiting with nothing to do, would be higher than the map's count. You can test a different number with "Couriers on duty" under More settings.
